# Human Architecture and Ship-Scale Environments

Status: initial integration rules

## Architecture principle

Human construction should remain legible as engineered, maintainable, pressurized technology, but its form must respond to the world it occupies.

The project should avoid the visual impression that every settlement was designed in a neutral CAD environment and then dropped onto arbitrary terrain.

## Environmental adaptation checklist

Every major settlement/POI should answer:

- Why was this exact site selected?
- What environmental force most threatens the structure?
- What part of the facility must be protected most aggressively?
- Where do ships approach and land?
- How are cargo, people, heat, waste, power, and air moved?
- What local material or geology affects construction?
- What does abandonment/damage look like here specifically?

## Examples of site-driven architecture

### Crater-wall installation

- structure recessed into rock for radiation/impact protection;
- hangars open laterally into the crater;
- defense batteries positioned along rim lines;
- elevators/tunnels connect vertical levels;
- exterior habitation volume minimized;
- approach path naturally follows crater geometry.

### Abrasive storm world

- low-profile structures behind windbreak formations;
- sacrificial exterior panels;
- protected intakes;
- recessed landing zones;
- maintenance tunnels rather than exposed walkways;
- sensor masts positioned only where necessary.

### Dense-atmosphere world

- broad structural bracing;
- elevated platforms above unstable ground;
- short-range navigation beacons;
- large lighting/marker systems for low visibility;
- potentially buoyant or suspended infrastructure where justified.

### Ocean world

- pylons, floating platforms, cliff anchors, tidal protection;
- hangars facing sheltered water/air corridors;
- resource piping and desalination visible as actual infrastructure.

## Ship-scale environment doctrine

Low-altitude ship flight should influence world design from the beginning rather than being added after ground environments are complete.

A world should contain three overlapping scales:

1. **Orbital read** — continents, major storms, rings, large impact structures, color/material zones.
2. **Ship read** — canyons, craters, cliffs, arches, installations, asteroid cavities, approach corridors.
3. **On-foot read** — rooms, paths, vegetation, cover, local hazards, interiors.

A strong showcase location should transition naturally through all three.

## Flight route types

The project should intentionally author spaces where piloting matters:

- crater-wall descents;
- trench/canyon runs;
- fractured plate gaps;
- arch fields;
- lava/thermal valleys;
- cliff-side approaches;
- asteroid tunnels;
- hollow-body entrances;
- storm-limited instrument routes;
- low passes under or between giant environmental forms.

These should not become arcade racetracks everywhere. They are environmental opportunities that can support exploration, combat, smuggling, assault, or discovery.

## Route readability

Ship-scale routes need visual grammar.

Useful cues include:

- lighting chains;
- geological alignment;
- radar/sensor signatures;
- damaged navigation markers;
- distant silhouettes;
- defensive orientation;
- traffic paths;
- dust/plume behavior;
- openings that reveal deeper spaces.

The player should often be able to infer "something is down there" without a floating quest marker.

## Concealed locations

Hidden locations should be concealed spatially rather than merely disabled on the map.

Examples:

- pirate hangar behind a crater lip;
- mining complex inside an asteroid shadow;
- abandoned lab inside a giant fracture;
- smuggler dock beneath a floating rock shelf;
- settlement hidden under permanent storm cover;
- derelict station embedded in ring debris.

Discovery can involve sight, sensors, intercepted traffic, heat signatures, artificial lighting, or unusual flight paths.

## Surface defenses

If ship flight exists near POIs, some environments should respond at ship scale.

Potential systems:

- radar-guided turrets;
- missile batteries;
- point-defense emplacements;
- shield generators;
- launch bays for defending craft;
- sensor jammers;
- terrain-masked firing positions.

The location should visually explain how those defenses cover the approach.

## Landing integration

Landing zones should stop feeling like generic circles.

Possible landing contexts:

- excavated pad inside crater wall;
- rough natural shelf used by smugglers;
- vertical hangar slot;
- industrial gantry;
- sheltered canyon basin;
- asteroid docking cavity;
- ocean platform;
- field landing on naturally suitable terrain.

## Ground/ship continuity

The ideal gameplay sequence is:

```text
see landmark from distance
-> fly toward it
-> use terrain to approach
-> encounter defenses/activity
-> select landing or ingress point
-> land
-> leave the same ship
-> continue into the location on foot
```

Even where engine transitions must be hidden technically, the visual logic should preserve this continuity.

## Reuse rule

Reusable building modules are encouraged, but their assembly should respond to site constraints.

A module kit becomes a design language, not a repeated level.

The same pressure-hab module could appear:

- embedded horizontally in a cliff;
- stacked vertically in low gravity;
- partially buried under regolith;
- connected to a floating platform;
- shielded behind mineral ribs;
- damaged and exposed after erosion.

This is preferable to building hundreds of unique modules with no systemic placement logic.
