# Visual Philosophy

Status: initial visual baseline

## Core premise

Human technology may remain recognizably human, practical, industrial, and broadly compatible with Starfield's NASA-punk foundation. The universe around it should become far less familiar.

The contrast is deliberate:

> believable human machinery operating in places that do not feel designed around human expectations.

The project should not become fantasy-space for its own sake. Alienness should emerge from coherent physical and ecological rules, not arbitrary decoration.

## Primary visual principles

### 1. Structure before palette

A world must remain distinctive when desaturated.

Color can strengthen identity, but it may not carry identity by itself. Terrain silhouette, atmospheric depth, flora geometry, architecture, scale, and spatial composition should do most of the work.

### 2. Planetary identity is systemic

Important worlds should have a small number of strong physical rules that affect many systems at once.

Examples:

- dense atmosphere changes visibility, flora, aircraft behavior, architecture, weather, and sound;
- tidal locking changes climate, settlement position, ecology, lighting, migration, and travel routes;
- extreme erosion changes geology, shelter, construction, ruins, resource extraction, and combat spaces.

A world should not feel like a pile of unrelated alien assets.

### 3. Familiarity is allowed, but not default

Earth-like valleys, forests, oceans, clouds, and temperate climates are valid. They become more meaningful when uncommon.

The universe should contain a broad spectrum:

- sterile and violently hostile worlds;
- worlds with strange geology but no life;
- chemically or atmospherically unusual worlds;
- life-bearing worlds with unfamiliar ecological structures;
- rare worlds with recognizably Earth-like environmental logic.

### 4. Scale must support wonder and gameplay

Large forms should be large enough to affect navigation.

A crater is not merely background dressing if the player can descend into it, hide behind its walls, discover a base inside it, and fight through its terrain.

A plant family intended to create an alien forest should alter sight lines, traversal, cover, and ship approach rather than merely populate the ground.

### 5. Environmental storytelling begins at landscape scale

A location should make visual sense before the player reads a terminal.

The environment should reveal:

- what forces shaped it;
- where water, heat, wind, radiation, or life concentrate;
- why humans settled there;
- what failed;
- how the current occupants adapted.

### 6. Human structures belong to the site

Avoid the impression that prefabs were lowered from orbit onto arbitrary terrain.

Buildings should respond to:

- gravity;
- prevailing weather;
- temperature;
- corrosive chemistry;
- radiation;
- seismic activity;
- terrain geometry;
- available local material;
- tactical exposure;
- landing and logistics requirements.

### 7. Repetition must be hidden by meaning, not cosmetics

Reusing modules is acceptable. Reusing an identical experience is not.

A repeated structural kit should produce different results through layout, orientation, damage, additions, surrounding terrain, faction adaptation, environmental constraints, and history.

### 8. Space and surface share one visual language

Planetary surfaces, orbital silhouettes, moons, rings, asteroid fields, stations, and ship-scale POIs should visually relate.

A planet's geology should influence its moon debris, mining economy, orbital infrastructure, settlement materials, and even the kinds of hidden locations around it where practical.

## Human technology baseline

The overhaul should generally retain Starfield's strength in readable, grounded industrial design:

- exposed functional machinery;
- pressure doors and seals;
- practical hab modules;
- believable maintenance access;
- utilitarian cockpits;
- modular spacecraft construction;
- visible logistics and life-support needs.

The goal is not to erase this style. The goal is to place it against worlds that are dramatically more varied.

Human technology may evolve visually where an environment demands it. A sulfur-storm outpost should not use exactly the same exterior treatment as a temperate colony.

## Composition targets

Important locations should usually have a readable hierarchy:

1. planetary or regional silhouette;
2. dominant landmark;
3. route or approach language;
4. local environmental structure;
5. architecture/POI integration;
6. detail layer.

This helps prevent the common procedural failure where every square meter receives equal visual noise.

## The Morrowind lesson

The useful lesson from Morrowind is not "use giant mushrooms."

It is that a world becomes memorable when geology, culture, architecture, ecology, travel, and atmosphere reinforce one another strongly enough that the player develops a mental map of places that could not easily belong elsewhere.

That principle should guide this project more than literal imitation of any Morrowind asset or biome.

## Visual success test

For each important world, ask:

- Can it be recognized from its horizon alone?
- Can it be recognized in grayscale?
- Does its settlement architecture appear adapted to the environment?
- Does the environment create gameplay consequences?
- Does it contain at least one memorable scale-defining landmark?
- Would moving a vanilla POI into the scene obviously look wrong?
- Can ship flight reveal aspects of the world that walking cannot?

If most answers are no, the world is not differentiated enough.
