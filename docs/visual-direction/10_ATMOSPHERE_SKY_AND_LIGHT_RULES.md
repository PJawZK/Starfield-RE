# Atmosphere, Sky, and Light Rules

Status: project-wide visual physics rule

## Purpose

Sky color, haze, cloud behavior, horizon scattering, weather, and apparent daylight must come from the world's atmosphere and star rather than from a default Earth-like art treatment.

A blue sky is not the neutral condition. It is one particular result of atmospheric composition, pressure, aerosols, stellar spectrum, and viewing geometry.

## Core rule

**Every visible sky must be justified by the planetary atmosphere.**

Before assigning a sky treatment, establish at minimum:

- surface pressure;
- dominant atmospheric gases;
- aerosol/dust content;
- cloud-forming species if any;
- stellar spectrum and apparent solar intensity;
- altitude and viewing geometry.

The visual treatment then follows from those physical causes.

## Airless and near-airless worlds

For bodies such as Khepri:

- no blue atmospheric sky;
- no ordinary clouds;
- no diffuse atmospheric haze unless a transient local particulate event supplies it;
- the sky is effectively black even in daylight;
- terrain can still be brightly illuminated by direct stellar light;
- shadows are hard and high-contrast;
- horizon glow must be extremely limited unless dust, vapor, plume material, or an artificial envelope is present;
- lightning and conventional weather are absent unless a specifically justified local phenomenon produces them.

Localized dust lofting, impact ejecta, sublimation vapor, volcanic or industrial plumes, or anomaly-driven particulate behavior may temporarily create visible material against the black sky, but this is not equivalent to a global atmosphere.

## Thin atmospheres

Thin atmospheres may produce:

- weak horizon scattering;
- muted or non-blue skies;
- strong direct-light dominance;
- localized dust or ice hazes;
- highly altitude-dependent appearance.

Do not automatically increase saturation or make the sky blue merely because some atmosphere exists.

## Dense atmospheres

Dense atmospheres can support stronger scattering, haze, clouds, storms, and diffuse light, but appearance must still derive from chemistry and aerosols.

Possible sky families include:

- pale blue or cyan;
- gray-white;
- yellow/tan dust scattering;
- orange/red aerosol-dominated light;
- deep blue under specific atmospheric conditions;
- near-white high-opacity haze;
- layered colored scattering when justified by composition and star spectrum.

Color should not be used as arbitrary alien decoration.

## High volcanic activity

High volcanic or tectonic activity does not automatically imply a blue sky.

Where volcanism strongly affects the atmosphere, expect some combination of:

- ash or mineral aerosols;
- sulfur-bearing haze;
- reduced visibility;
- warm or dirty-gray scattering;
- local plume columns;
- strong regional variation;
- temporary darkening or reddening of stellar light.

A clear blue sky on a highly active volcanic world requires an explicit atmospheric reason rather than being the visual default.

## Exceptional local pockets

Localized Earth-like or otherwise unusual sky conditions are allowed when caused by a specific mechanism.

Examples:

- a naturally enclosed basin with unusual pressure/composition;
- a deep cavern or caldera containing a trapped atmosphere;
- a stable local vapor layer;
- a human-maintained environmental envelope;
- a sealed or semi-sealed megastructure with artificial weather and lighting;
- alien infrastructure maintaining local atmospheric conditions;
- an anomaly that has a defined physical effect on pressure, gases, or light.

These pockets should be visibly bounded or otherwise legible as exceptions. They must not silently redefine the entire planet.

## Human and alien intervention

Advanced civilizations may deliberately alter local atmospheric conditions, but intervention should remain engineering-readable.

Possible interventions include:

- pressurized valley or crater settlements;
- electromagnetic or structural containment;
- atmospheric processing plants;
- climate-managed habitat shells;
- aerosol management;
- artificial cloud/water cycles inside large habitats;
- localized terraforming experiments.

The result may look startlingly Earth-like inside a hostile world precisely because it is exceptional.

## Lighting rule

Sky color and terrain lighting are separate questions.

Even with a black sky, a sunlit airless surface can be extremely bright. Conversely, a dense atmosphere can produce a luminous sky while heavily attenuating direct sunlight.

Benchmark art must therefore distinguish:

- direct stellar illumination;
- atmospheric scattering;
- surface reflectance;
- particulate/plume scattering;
- artificial light.

## Visual anti-patterns

Avoid:

- blue sky as the default for any world with an atmosphere;
- Earth-like clouds without a plausible condensable species and pressure/temperature regime;
- dramatic lightning on an airless body;
- atmospheric fog used only to add cinematic depth where no atmosphere exists;
- warm sunset grading applied universally;
- colored skies chosen only to make a world look alien;
- local atmospheric exceptions shown without a cause or visible boundary.

## Benchmark requirement

Every world sheet and concept benchmark should explicitly state:

1. atmospheric pressure class;
2. dominant sky/scattering behavior;
3. whether ordinary clouds can exist;
4. whether weather can exist;
5. what local exceptions are allowed;
6. what the sky should look like under neutral daylight.

If a concept image violates these rules, its composition or terrain may still be useful, but it is not valid visual evidence for sky/atmosphere treatment.
